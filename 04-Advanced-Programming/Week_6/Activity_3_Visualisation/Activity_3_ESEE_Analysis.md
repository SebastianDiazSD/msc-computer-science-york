# Activity 3 – Impact Contexts: ESEE Analysis

**Chosen case studies:** Bias in Algorithms + Driverless Cars  
**Framework:** Social | Environmental | Economic | Educational — from local and global perspectives.

---

## Case Study A: Bias in Algorithms

### Social
**Local:** At a community level, algorithmic bias in tools like predictive policing software (e.g., PredPol, deployed in several UK and US cities) concentrates police resources in historically over-policed communities, creating a feedback loop that produces more crime records in those areas — regardless of actual crime rates. At the individual level, a biased hiring algorithm may prevent a qualified candidate from even reaching a human recruiter.

**Global:** The deployment of biased facial recognition by governments in authoritarian states raises concerns about mass surveillance and the erosion of civil liberties at a global scale. The same technology that might unlock a phone or identify a missing person can be used to track dissidents. Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) found commercial facial recognition systems from major vendors had error rates up to 34.7% higher for darker-skinned women than lighter-skinned men, meaning the systems are least reliable for the groups who face the most risk from their misuse.

**Benefit:** Algorithms do enable genuinely positive social outcomes when designed carefully — automated screening tools can reduce *human* bias in recruitment if built and monitored properly. The goal should be better-designed systems, not rejection of the technology.

---

### Environmental
**Local:** The computational infrastructure required to train and run large ML models — data centres, GPU clusters, cooling systems — has a significant local energy footprint. A data centre in a water-stressed region may compete with communities for water resources used in cooling.

**Global:** Training large language and vision models produces substantial CO₂ emissions. Patterson et al. (2021) estimated that training GPT-3 produced approximately 502 tonnes of CO₂e. As these systems proliferate globally and are retrained regularly, aggregate emissions from ML infrastructure become environmentally significant. The EU AI Act's push toward smaller, more efficient models for lower-risk applications has a meaningful environmental dimension.

**Potential benefit:** Well-designed algorithmic systems for energy grid management, climate modelling, and logistics optimisation can reduce overall environmental harm. The net impact depends heavily on what the systems are used for and how their infrastructure is powered.

---

### Economic
**Local:** In a city where automated credit scoring algorithms are used by banks, a biased algorithm can systematically deny loans to residents of certain postcodes — a practice called *redlining* (historically a human-driven discrimination, now replicated algorithmically). This depresses local investment and reinforces poverty traps.

**Global:** The concentration of AI development in a small number of tech companies — primarily US- and China-based — means that the economic value generated by these systems flows predominantly to shareholders in those countries. The communities whose data trains these systems (often in lower-income countries) receive little economic benefit. O'Neil (2016) describes this as a "feedback loop of injustice" that algorithmic systems systematise and scale.

**Economic opportunity:** Algorithmic systems also create real economic value — more accurate fraud detection, optimised supply chains, and personalised medicine. The question is who captures that value and who bears the cost of errors.

---

### Educational
**Local:** Schools and universities are increasingly using algorithmic systems for student assessment, admissions, and resource allocation. The 2020 A-level fiasco in the UK — where a government algorithm downgraded predicted grades, disproportionately affecting state school students — demonstrated how bias in educational algorithms directly harms individual students and widens existing inequality.

**Global:** For the next generation of computing students and practitioners, bias in algorithms represents a critical curriculum topic. Educators need to teach not just how to build ML models but how to measure, document, and mitigate bias — using frameworks like IBM's AI Fairness 360 toolkit or Google's What-If Tool. There is also a literacy challenge: members of the public who cannot critically evaluate algorithmic outputs are vulnerable to manipulation by filter bubbles and targeted misinformation.

**Lesson to pass on:** Algorithms are not neutral. Every design choice — what data to train on, what metric to optimise, what threshold to deploy at — encodes values. Future practitioners must treat fairness and explainability as first-class engineering requirements, not afterthoughts.

## Case Study B: Driverless Cars

### Social
**Local:** In cities where AV trials are underway (e.g., Waymo in San Francisco, Navya in Sion, Switzerland), residents experience direct social impacts: reduced pedestrian risk in trial zones, but also anxiety about unpredictable vehicle behaviour. People with disabilities — particularly those who cannot drive — stand to gain significantly from reliable AV services. Families who lose relatives to road accidents caused by human error may see AVs as a social benefit.

**Global:** The displacement of professional drivers is one of the most significant social impacts at scale. The International Labour Organization estimates that 3.5 million truck drivers in the US alone could be displaced by AV technology. This impact would fall disproportionately on workers without university education — widening existing inequality.

**Benefit:** The WHO estimates that 1.35 million people die in road accidents each year globally. If AVs reduce human error (responsible for ~94% of accidents), the social benefit in lives saved could be enormous.

---

### Environmental
**Local:** Electric AVs in urban environments could substantially reduce localised air pollution — a major contributor to respiratory disease, particularly in low-income urban areas near arterial roads. San Francisco's Waymo fleet (all-electric) generates zero tailpipe emissions.

**Global:** However, the environmental benefit depends entirely on how the electricity powering AVs is generated. In countries where the grid remains largely coal-powered, mass EV/AV adoption merely shifts emissions from the exhaust pipe to the power station. Additionally, AV sensor hardware — LiDAR units, cameras, radar systems — require rare earth elements whose extraction carries significant environmental costs (cobalt, lithium, neodymium).

**Route optimisation benefit:** AVs operating in coordinated platoons can reduce aerodynamic drag by up to 15%, meaningfully improving fleet fuel efficiency at scale.

---

### Economic
**Local:** AV deployment will disrupt local economies built around transportation — taxi firms, driving schools, vehicle insurance, car parks (AVs need less parking due to fleet sharing models), and even traffic law enforcement. Cities must plan for these transitions to avoid localised economic shocks.

**Global:** The AV market is projected to be worth over $500 billion by 2030 (Allied Market Research). Most of this value flows to technology companies (Waymo, Tesla, Mobileye) and automotive manufacturers that have invested heavily in the technology. The transition creates winners (tech firms, shareholders) and losers (incumbent transport workers and businesses).

**Positive economic case:** AVs could significantly reduce the $340 billion annual cost of road accidents in the US (NHTSA), reduce logistics costs, and enable genuinely new mobility-as-a-service business models that give people without cars access to convenient transport.

---

### Educational
**Local:** The rise of AVs creates immediate demand for new technical skills — embedded systems programming, computer vision, sensor fusion, safety-critical software engineering — that few current vocational programmes address. Driving schools will need to adapt or may cease to exist. Traffic police will need training in investigating AV incidents.

**Global:** University engineering programmes globally need to integrate autonomous systems, safety-critical software, and AV ethics into curricula. The 2018 Uber fatality highlighted the risk of deploying systems designed by engineers who had not been trained to reason about safety-critical edge cases and the human cost of software failure.

**Lesson to pass on:** The engineering of safety-critical autonomous systems is fundamentally different from consumer software development. Failure modes are not bugs to be patched — they are accidents that injure and kill people. Future practitioners must understand formal verification, fault-tree analysis, and the ethical weight of design decisions in safety-critical contexts.
